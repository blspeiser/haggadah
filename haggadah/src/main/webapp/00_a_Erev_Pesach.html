עֶרֶב פֶּסַח Passover Eve
The Removal of Chametz
Formal preparations for Passover begin the night before the Seder, starting with the process for removing chametz from one's domain. This not only includes the location where one will be celebrating Passover, but from all locations in one's domain, such as a workplace, automobile, or alternate residences.
Because the obligation on Passover is to own no chametz, the sages instituted that one can sell their chametz before Passover, in order so that one will not actually own any remaining chametz in their house and will therefore fulfill the commandment of removing chametz from their domain. However, prior to this institution, the sages relied on active removal of chametz from one's ownership by destroying it. This process remains an essential activity prior to the onset of Passover. It begins with the search for the chametz, and then concludes with its destruction and nullification the next morning.
The search for chametz is traditionally conducted in the dark, using a candle for light. Many authorities permit the use of a flashlight. While the origin of this is likely historical - the invention of modern lighting is a recent phenomenon and so the use of a candle in the evening was simply a necessity - some claim that the increased contrast of the spotlight effect of a light in the dark makes the search more effective. Others claim it is metaphorical; that Passover should begin in darkness with only the first glimmer of light to show the way. Regardless of the reasoning, the search is still traditionally conducted in the dark to this day.
The purpose of this search is to find any last chametz that has not yet been discovered and destroy it, so that the location will be chametz-free ahead of the holiday. However, it is clear that by this time, most people have already sufficiently cleaned their houses for Passover and are unlikely to actually have random chametz remaining. In order to prevent the recitation of an unnecessary blessing, someone should hide chametz ahead of time, so that some will indeed be located during the search. Although this would technically only require hiding one piece of chametz, the tradition was established to hide ten pieces for the sake of providing a substantive ritual. While conducting the search, any unexpectedly discovered chametz should be gathered as well. Many use a feather as part of the search to sweep up the crumbs, even if only performatively.
Although the recited blessing specifically refers to the burning of chametz, this is only because burning is the simplest mechanism for achieving the end result of posessing no chametz.
After preparations for the search have been completed and the participants are ready to begin, recite the following blessing:
בָּרוּךְ אַתָּה יי אֱלֹהֵֽינוּ מֶֽלֶךְ הָעוֹלָם אֲשֶׁר קִדְּשָֽׁנוּ בְּמִצְוֹתָיו וְצִוָּֽנוּ עַל בִּעוּר חָמֵץ. Blessed are You, The Eternal our God, King of the Universe; Who has sanctified us with His commandments and has commanded us regarding the burning of chametz.
After all of the intentionally hidden chametz is found and the search is effectively completed, the found chametz is set aside for destruction the next morning and the following paragraph is recited.
This proclamation is the first stage of ensuring that no chametz remains in one's possession before beginning the Passover holiday. It should be noted that the passage is in Aramaic - not Hebrew - because it was intended to be proclaimed in the vernacular, and Aramaic was the common language of the time. Because the proclamation is driven by intention rather than ritual, it is recommended that all participants recite the following passage in whatever language they understand.
כׇּל חֲמִירָא וַחֲמִיעָא דְאִכָּא בִרְשׁוּתִי דְלָא חֲמִתֵּהּ וּדְלָא בְעַרְתֵּהּ לִבְטִיל וְלֶהֱוֵי הֶפְקֵר כְּעַפְרָא דְאַרְעָא. Any chametz, leavened products, or leavening agents in my possession that I have not seen, have not destroyed, or am not aware of - I hereby nullify, disown, and consider completely irrelevant to me; and carry no more significance to me than dust of the earth.
The remaining procedures for the removal of chametz continue the following morning.
The chametz gathered during the previous night's search, along with any other additional chametz still in one's posession that has not been sold or given away, should be destroyed, traditionally by burning it. However, if one is unable to burn it, any other means of destruction that removes it from the property is valid; such as feeding it to fish in a lake, disposal in a public garbage receptacle, and so on.
After the chametz has been destroyed (or even while it is burning), the following paragraph is recited.
Like the previous evening's proclamation, this is the most essential aspect of this entire process, for it fully certifies that there is no longer any chametz in one's possession, even if one can physically see it (such as in the case where it has been sold to a third party and, despite being visible, is no longer owned by the resident).
Even if one does not have any chametz to destroy, or even if one forgot to perform any previous step in the search for chametz, this paragraph should be recited. Similar to the previous proclamation, it is recommended that all participants recite the following passage in whatever language they understand.
כׇּל חֲמִירָא וַחֲמִיעָא דְאִכָּא בִרְשׁוּתִי דַחֲמִתֵּהּ וּדְלָא חֲמִתֵּהּ דְּבִעַרְתֵּהּ וּדְלָא בִעַרְתֵּהּ לִבְטִיל וְלֶהֱוֵי הֶפְקֵר כְּעַפְרָא דְאַרְעָא. Any chametz, leavened products, or leavening agents in my possession that I have seen or have not seen, have destroyed or have not destroyed, or am aware of or not aware of - I hereby nullify, disown, and consider completely irrelevant to me; and carry no more significance to me than dust of the earth.
With this final proclamation, one has fulfilled their obligation to remove any chametz from their domain prior to Passover.
Eiruv Tavshillin
According to the established Hebrew calendar, the first night of Passover can only fall on a Friday, Saturday, Monday, or Wednesday night. For Jews in the Diaspora who celebrate two full days of yom tov (the holiday portion of the festival), that means that when Passover begins on a Wednesday night, the holiday can directly lead into the Sabbath. On years when this occurs, one should make an eiruv tavshillin, a halachic designation that would lose meaning in translation.
On years when Passover does not directly lead into the Sabbath, an eiruv tavshillin is unnecessary and should not be designated.
Jewish law stipulates that one may not cook on the Sabbath, but one may cook on yom tov. However, even so, one may only prepare food for that same day of yom tov; not even for the second day of yom tov nor the day after the holiday is over. This is so that one does not get bogged down by excessive preparation over the holiday and demean the restful nature of the day; only what is necessary for that day's meals is permissible.
This presents a logistical problem whenever any holiday leads directly into the Sabbath: when will someone prepare food for the Sabbath, since all of the preceding days only allow food to be prepared for the same day's meals? Due to this logistical challenge, the sages instituted a halachic workaround called an eiruv tavshillin. This allows one to prepare food for Sabbath during the holiday by officially starting the Sabbath preparations prior to the onset of the holiday, and so any food preparation over the holiday is simply an extension of the preparation that was already started before the holiday began. This allows one to prepare food for the Sabbath on the holiday, but still limits one from preparing food for after the Sabbath is over.
Prior to the onset of Passover, one designates two or more cooked or baked foods that will specifically be eaten on the Sabbath as the eiruv tavshillin. Many people have a custom to use a piece of matzah and a hard-boiled egg (since it could be considered a bare minimum to meet the requirements of a meal), but in practice, one should choose cooked or baked foods that are intentionally going to be served at the Sabbath meal. Many people have the custom to eat the eiruv tavshillin specifically as part of their Seudat Shlishit, the Third Sabbath Meal, which is typically eaten in the late afternoon before the Sabbath ends.
While holding the cooked and/or baked foods, one should recite the following blessing and then the subsequent declaration. The declaration, originally written in the vernacular Aramaic, should ideally be recited in a language that one understands.
בָּרוּךְ אַתָּה יי אֱלֹהֵינוּ מֶלֶךְ הָעוֹלָם, אֲשֶׁר קִדְּשָׁנוּ בְּמִצְוֹתָיו וְצִוָּנוּ עַל מִצְוַת עֵרוּב. Blessed are You, The Eternal our God, King of the Universe; Who has sanctified us with His commandments and has commanded us regarding the mitzvah of eiruv.
בְּדֵין עֵרוּבָא, יְהֵא שָׁרֵי לָנָא לַאֲפוֹיֵי וּלְבַשּׁוּלֵי וּלְאַטְמוֹנֵי וּלְתַקּוּנֵי וּלְאַדְלוּקֵי שַׁרְגָּא וּלְמֶעְבַּד כָּל־צָרְכָּנָא, מִיּוֹם טוֹב לְשַׁבָּת. With this eiruv, it is permissible to bake, cook, insulate food, prepare utensils, light candles, and do whatever is needed for the Sabbath during the holiday.
Set aside the foods designated as the eiruv tavshillin and serve them during an upcoming Sabbath meal.
